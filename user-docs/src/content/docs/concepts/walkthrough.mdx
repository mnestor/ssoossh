A friendly guide to certificate-based SSH login: the same flow as
[interactive user certificates](/ssoossh/concepts/user-certificate/), told
from the outside, one screen at a time. Nothing here is configuration. If you
have just been told your company uses ssoossh, start here.

Open your terminal and run `ssoossh ssh login`, or just `ssh host` if it is set
up in your `ssh_config`.

If you already hold a valid certificate, the client reuses it and you are done
with no browser at all. Otherwise it generates a fresh, unique keypair, sends
the public key to the ssoossh server, and gets back a URL.

Your terminal prints that URL and then waits patiently. Nothing leaves your
laptop except the public key: the private key stays right there, on your
machine.

ssoossh tries to open the URL in your default browser. If that does not work,
or you would rather do it yourself, paste the link into a browser by hand.

You land on the ssoossh login page, which immediately sends you to your
company's identity provider: the same place you log into everything else.

Nothing special happens here. You log in exactly the way you normally do,
including any multi-factor step your company requires.

After logging in you land on an approval page. This is where ssoossh shows you
the certificate it is about to issue.

In green you see what you have been granted: which servers you can log into,
and how long the certificate lasts. In amber you see anything you asked for
that policy does not allow, such as file transfer or agent forwarding.

That difference is the point. You can see what you requested against what you
are actually getting, and if something looks wrong you can decline and ask your
administrator to adjust the policy.

You click Approve. That is it for you: the browser shows a confirmation page
and you are done.

Meanwhile your terminal has not gone anywhere. It is still sitting on that
event stream, waiting for the certificate to arrive. The server signs the
certificate in the background and delivers it straight to the waiting client,
so the terminal learns the outcome the moment the certificate lands. The
certificate is never stored on the server, so that delivery is the only copy.

Within seconds your terminal wakes up. The server has signed your certificate:
a visitor badge that says who you are, which login names (principals) you may
use, and when it expires.

ssoossh loads that certificate into your local ssh-agent, the part of SSH that
holds your credentials, or writes it to key files if you do not run an agent.
The private key never left your laptop; the certificate just joins it.

You are now ready to connect. The whole flow takes about a minute, including
the time you spent logging in.

Now you run `ssh prod-server.internal` the way you normally would. SSH uses the
certificate you just received, your visitor badge, to authenticate.

The server does not have your key stored anywhere. It trusts the badge office,
which is ssoossh's CA key. It checks the certificate's signature, checks that
the principals and constraints match what you are trying to do, and if
everything holds, you are in.

No special credentials to manage, no key stored in a thousand places, no
ancient key from years ago that nobody remembers.

Your certificate is short-lived on purpose. After its window closes it is
worthless. Nobody has to revoke it and nobody has to rotate anything; it just
stops working.

Next time you need a server, run the command again. You go through the same
flow, get a new badge, and keep working. It stays out of your way because
ssoossh reuses a certificate that has not expired yet, so you see the browser
only when a new one is needed: typically once a workday, not once per
connection.

That is the whole story. A certificate is a visitor badge, issued for a
moment, checked at the door, and discarded when its time is up.

:::note
Twelve hours is the example above, not a default. How long your certificate
lasts is set by your administrator, and can vary by group and by the network
you are on -- see
[options and lifetime resolution](/ssoossh/concepts/options-and-lifetime/).
:::
