How ssoossh Works

A friendly guide to certificate-based SSH login

Step 1

You type the command

Open your terminal and run ssoossh ssh login (or just ssh host if you've set it up in your config).

The ssoossh client immediately generates a fresh, unique key pair for this request. It then sends the public key to the ssoossh server and gets back a URL.

Your terminal prints that URL and then waits patiently. Nothing leaves your laptop yet except the public key — the private key stays right there, locked to your machine.

Step 2

Your browser opens automatically

ssoossh tries to open the URL in your default browser. If that doesn't work (or you just prefer to do it yourself), you can paste the link into your browser manually.

You land on the ssoossh login page, which immediately sends you to your company's identity provider — the same place you log into Slack, Jira, or whatever else your company uses.

Nothing special here. You log in exactly the way you normally do, including any multi-factor authentication your company requires.

Step 3

You see exactly what you're approving

After logging in, you land on an approval page. This is where ssoossh shows you the certificate it's about to issue.

In green, you see what you've been granted: which servers you can log into, how long the certificate lasts. In amber, you see anything you asked for that the policy doesn't allow — things like file transfer, or agent forwarding.

This is genuinely valuable. You can see the difference between what you requested and what you're actually getting, and if something looks wrong, you can decline and ask your administrator to adjust the policy.

Step 4

You approve, and it's done on your end

You click Approve. That's it for you — the browser shows a confirmation page and you're done.

Meanwhile, your terminal hasn't gone anywhere. It's still sitting patiently on that SSE connection, waiting for the certificate to arrive. ssoossh did this on purpose: the waiting client gets the signal that approval happened right away, even before the certificate is fully signed and delivered.

Step 5

Your certificate arrives, automatically

Within seconds, your terminal wakes up. The server has signed your certificate — a visitor badge that says exactly who you are, which servers you can visit, and when it expires.

ssoossh loads this certificate into your local ssh-agent (the part of SSH that holds your credentials). The private key never left your laptop; the certificate just joins it in the agent.

You're now ready to connect. The whole flow took maybe a minute, including the time you spent logging in.

Step 6

SSH connects and works immediately

Now you run ssh prod-server.internal like you normally would. SSH uses the certificate you just received — your "visitor badge" — to authenticate.

The server doesn't have your key stored anywhere. It just trusts the badge office (ssoossh's CA key). It checks the certificate's signature, verifies that the principals and constraints match what you're trying to do, and if everything looks good, you're in.

No special credentials to manage, no key stored in a thousand places, no ancient key from years ago that nobody remembers.

Step 7

It expires on its own, by design

Your certificate is short-lived on purpose — in this case, 12 hours. After that time, it's worthless. Nobody has to revoke it, nobody has to rotate anything. It just stops working.

Next time you need to access a server, just run the command again. You'll go through the same flow, get a new badge, and keep working. It's frictionless because ssoossh caches certificates that haven't expired yet, so you only see the browser once per session.

That's the whole story. A certificate is a visitor badge: issued for a moment, checked at the door, and discarded when its time is up.

ssoossh is open-source SSH over your existing identity provider. Learn more at github.com/mnestor/ssoossh
